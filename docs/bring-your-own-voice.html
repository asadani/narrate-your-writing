Bring Your Own Voice

Cloning your own voice from a 4.5-second recording and narrating 50 minutes of writing with it — on a four-gigabyte laptop GPU, entirely offline, with Apache-2.0 weights. The hard parts are not the ones you expect.

Part one covers the simpler path — a good synthetic voice, no GPU, no recording — in Kokoro Narration Pipeline. Start there if you don't specifically need your own voice.

4 GB is the binding constraint, and it shapes every decision below

The weights take 2.2 GB, leaving under 1.8 GB for everything else. Almost every counterintuitive choice in this guide — the short reference, the batch size, the dtype — exists because of that ceiling. On a 24 GB card most of it stops mattering.

Recording the reference

This is where the real difficulty lives, and it has nothing to do with machine learning. Three separate attempts were needed to get a usable 90 seconds, and the failures were all about capture hardware.

1

Write a passage that covers your prosody

Don't read random text. Cover the shapes the model has to reproduce: long subordinate clauses, a semicolon's mid-sentence lift, a colon setting up a list, two very short declaratives for the falling cadence, digits spoken in sequence, and at least one real question. Miss the question mark and every question in your output comes out flat.

READ THIS ALOUD. Narration pace - as if teaching, not chatting.
Leave a second of silence before you start and after you finish.
Mistakes are fine. Do not re-record to make it perfect.

A protocol is just an agreement about how two programs talk.

The model does not run the tool. It cannot.

Zero, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine.

Is it going to rain in Pune tomorrow?

Around 160 words, or 75–90 seconds at teaching pace. Read it at your normal speed — the clone copies your rhythm, and a carefully-slow reading produces a carefully-slow narrator for every chapter. Don't restart on a stumble; a fluffed word costs nothing, three increasingly stiff takes cost you the voice.

2

Do not record on a Bluetooth headset

The single most expensive lesson here. When a Bluetooth device is used as a microphone, the link drops from A2DP to the hands-free profile, which caps the microphone at 16 kHz sampling — an 8 kHz audio ceiling. Windows then upsamples to 48 kHz to fill the WAV header, so the file metadata looks perfect and the audio is not.

# the file claims 48 kHz stereo PCM...
samplerate: 48000 Hz   channels: 2   subtype: PCM_16

# ...but the spectrum stops dead
   4000-8000  Hz : 26.71%
   8000-12000 Hz :  0.04%     <- nothing above 8 kHz
  12000-16000 Hz :  0.02%

Above 8 kHz is where sibilance and consonant air live — the s, t, f, sh sounds. A clone conditioned on band-limited audio inherits that dullness. Built-in laptop array microphones frequently have the same problem for a different reason: a 16 kHz voice-communications DSP you cannot switch off.

Record on your phone

Any modern phone voice-memo app captures genuinely full-band 44.1 or 48 kHz and bypasses your laptop's audio pipeline entirely. Hold it a hand's width away, slightly off-axis so plosives don't hit the capsule directly. It will beat both your headset and your built-in mic.

3

Measure the recording before you trust it

Twenty lines that will save you an hour. Run this on every take:

import numpy as np, soundfile as sf

d, sr = sf.read("reference.wav")
if d.ndim > 1: d = d.mean(1)

peak  = float(np.max(np.abs(d)))
rms   = float(np.sqrt(np.mean(d**2)))
win   = int(sr * 0.05)
frames = [d[i:i+win] for i in range(0, len(d)-win, win)]
floor = float(np.percentile([np.sqrt(np.mean(f**2)) for f in frames], 5))

print(f"duration {len(d)/sr:.1f}s   rate {sr} Hz")
print(f"peak {peak:.3f}   clipped {int(np.sum(np.abs(d) > 0.999))}")
print(f"SNR approx {20*np.log10(rms/max(floor,1e-9)):.1f} dB")

# and the one that catches the Bluetooth trap:
S = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(d * np.hanning(len(d))))
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(d), 1/sr)
c = np.cumsum(S) / S.sum()
print(f"99.9% of energy below {f[np.searchsorted(c, 0.999)]:.0f} Hz")

What you want:

  • Duration 60 s or more — you'll use far less, but it gives you clean sentences to choose from
  • Peak below 0.99, zero clipped samples
  • SNR above roughly 25 dB — a quiet room matters more than a good microphone
  • 99.9% of energy below ~15 kHz, not below 8 kHz. If that number reads 7,900, you have the band-limit problem and no amount of resampling will fix it
4

Then throw most of it away

The most counterintuitive result of the whole exercise. Qwen3-TTS clones from as little as three seconds, and on a small card a short reference is dramatically better — not just acceptable:

ReferenceRTFPeak VRAMOutcome
85.1 s (full passage)9.7×3.90 GBthrashed, then stalled
4.52 s (one sentence)4.6×2.68 GBstable, no paging

At the model's 12 Hz token rate, 85 seconds of reference is roughly 1,000 acoustic tokens sitting in the KV cache for the entire generation, before a single word of output. On a 4 GB card that is the difference between computing and paging. Quality was indistinguishable by ear.

Pick one clean, complete sentence, cut on silence at both ends, and save it with an exact transcript beside it.

Installing, on modest hardware

5

Install, then repair the torch that pip gives you

conda create -n qwen-tts python=3.12 -y -c conda-forge --override-channels
conda activate qwen-tts
pip install -U qwen-tts

python -c "import torch; print(torch.__version__, torch.version.cuda)"
# 2.13.0+cpu  None        <- the trap

Installing the package resolves a CPU-only torch wheel, silently. Reinstall the same version from a CUDA index, and verify your card's architecture is actually in the build:

pip install --force-reinstall torch==2.13.0 torchaudio \
    --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cu126

python -c "import torch; print(torch.cuda.get_arch_list())"
# ['sm_50','sm_60','sm_61','sm_70','sm_75','sm_80','sm_86','sm_90']

Turing is sm_75. Prefer CUDA 12.6 over the CUDA 13 line if you are on an older card — CUDA 13 dropped several architectures, and checking get_arch_list() takes two seconds against an hour of confusing failures.

6

Two settings the official example gets wrong for old GPUs

model = Qwen3TTSModel.from_pretrained(
    "Qwen/Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-0.6B-Base",
    device_map="cuda:0",
    dtype=torch.bfloat16,          # NOT float16 - see below
    attn_implementation="sdpa",    # flash_attention_2 needs Ampere+
)

fp16 looks like the obvious optimisation. It crashes.

Turing has no native bf16, so fp16 is the faster path and the tempting choice. But the model was trained in bf16, whose exponent range is far wider; in fp16 the logits overflow inside the sampling loop, sampling returns an out-of-range index, and the embedding lookup fires a CUDA device-side assert that takes the process with it. Emulated bf16 is slower and correct. Take correct.

7

Batch two — and no more

Decoding is memory-bandwidth bound, so batching normally wins big. On a small card it wins, then stops:

BatchRTFPeak VRAM
14.4×3.38 GBfits
22.7×3.77 GBfits — 1.6× faster
42.8×5.07 GBover the card, pages

Batch 4 exceeds 4 GB, spills to system memory over PCIe, and hands back everything it gained. Find your own ceiling by watching peak VRAM, not by watching the clock.

Batch across a whole chapter, not within a paragraph — most paragraphs are a single chunk, so per-paragraph batching almost never fills a pair.

Making a three-hour run survivable

A long render will be interrupted. Ours was, five times: a GPU driver reset, a loose charger, sleep-on-battery, a background Windows update, and a task timeout. Design for it rather than hoping.

8

Cache every chunk to disk as it is made

The single highest-value change in the whole pipeline. Key on the chunk text plus the reference, write atomically, and an interruption costs you the chunk in flight instead of the chapter:

def say_many(self, texts):
    out, todo = [None] * len(texts), []
    for i, text in enumerate(texts):
        path = self._path(text)
        if path is not None and path.exists():
            try:
                out[i] = np.load(path); self.hits += 1; continue
            except Exception:
                path.unlink(missing_ok=True)   # truncated by a crash
        todo.append(i)

    for start in range(0, len(todo), self.batch):
        idxs = todo[start:start + self.batch]
        for i, samples in zip(idxs, self._generate([texts[i] for i in idxs])):
            out[i] = samples
            path = self._path(texts[i])
            if path is not None:
                tmp = path.with_suffix(".part")
                with open(tmp, "wb") as fh:     # np.save appends .npy
                    np.save(fh, samples)        # to a bare name - pass
                tmp.replace(path)               # a handle instead

Write to a temporary name and rename, so a crash mid-write can't leave a half file that poisons the next run. In practice this turned five interruptions into a few seconds of redone work, total.

9

Fall back to batch-of-one on OOM

except torch.cuda.OutOfMemoryError:
    torch.cuda.empty_cache()
    if n == 1:
        raise
    out = []                       # chunk lengths vary; an occasional
    for one in batch:              # pair won't fit even though the
        out.extend(self._generate([one]))   # average does
    return out
10

Refuse to write into your finished audio

If you already have a working narration, a clone run must not be able to destroy it. Make that structural, not careful:

if clone and out_dir.resolve() == AUDIO.resolve():
    sys.exit("Refusing to write clone output into %s -- that holds the "
             "finished course. Pick another --out-dir." % AUDIO)

Different engines write to different directories, and only the original engine patches the HTML. Both voices then coexist and you can compare them chapter by chapter before deciding what ships.

11

Check your machine won't sleep

powercfg /change standby-timeout-ac 0
powercfg /change standby-timeout-dc 0
powercfg /change hibernate-timeout-ac 0    # default was 3 hours

The idle timer counts user input, not GPU load, so an unattended render does not keep the machine awake. A three-hour hibernate default will kill a three-hour job at the worst possible moment. Set them back afterwards.

Chatterbox's 4.30 GB peak against a 4.00 GB card is the whole story of why it was slow: it paged constantly, its cost grew superlinearly with utterance length (11.6× at four seconds, 24.2× at eighteen), and it eventually triggered a GPU driver reset. Qwen holds 4.4× at five seconds and 4.5× at eighteen — flat — because it never leaves the card.

Expect the clone to run about 10% longer than a synthetic voice

Our 45.5-minute Kokoro track came out at 50.3 minutes cloned, chapter by chapter, +6% to +20%. Nothing was truncated — it simply speaks at a human pace, because it learned one. If your player uses a duration manifest, regenerate it.

Is it worth it?

Honestly: only if the voice being yours matters. Part one gets you a good narration in 45 minutes of CPU time with no recording, no GPU, and no traps. This path costs an afternoon of debugging, three hours of GPU time per full render, and a careful recording session.

But it is genuinely your voice, it runs entirely offline on hardware you already own, the weights are Apache 2.0, and nothing is uploaded to anyone. For a course, a personal blog, or anything where the narrator being a real person is part of the point, that is a different product — not a cheaper version of the same one.
